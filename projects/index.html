Echo chambers from social influence and unfollowing

Individual project · Physics of Complex Systems course, M.Sc. Physics, Sapienza · 2025/26

Question
Can echo chambers emerge on a social network without fake news or complex cognitive biases, only from two simple behaviours: being influenced by like-minded posts, and unfollowing people we disagree with?
Data and methods

Python reproduction of the agent-based model of Sasahara et al. (2021). Each agent holds an opinion and reads only the last few messages from the accounts it follows. It moves its opinion towards concordant messages, and with some probability unfollows the author of a discordant one and follows someone else.

Opinion dynamics coupled to network rewiring, scaled to 104 agents. Parameter sweeps with ensemble averaging, and tests that switch off one mechanism at a time (influence alone, rewiring alone). Every run is seeded, so results are reproducible.

Key result

The model is run until the network splits into internally converged, disconnected communities. TODO: key result of the reproduction to be added.

Tools
Python: NumPy, pandas, SciPy, networkx, matplotlib, seaborn.
Links
Code on GitHub · Slides

In plain words: A computer simulation of a social network shows whether people can end up in separate bubbles just by listening to those who agree with them and dropping those who do not.

Actionable insight: Testing a model by switching off one mechanism at a time shows which mechanism is really responsible for an outcome.

Cytoplasm partitioning and cell-cycle growth in Jurkat cells

Research internship, Istituto Italiano di Tecnologia (IIT), Center for Life Nano Science, Rome · 2025

Question

Is the cytoplasm split symmetrically between daughter cells? And how do cells control their size along the cell cycle: sizer, timer or adder?

Data

Quantitative flow cytometry on Jurkat cells (a human T-cell lymphoma line). An aliquot of about 106 cells sampled from the culture at each time point, 11 time points per week over two independent weeks, live and fixed (DAPI) samples, and several channels per cell: size, internal complexity, proliferation dye and DNA content.

The raw measurements belong to the host laboratory; the public repository includes a synthetic data generator with known ground truth.

Methods
  • Data cleaning and quality control: gating to remove debris, aggregates and doublets.
  • Overlapping populations: a Gaussian mixture model fitted by EM (purpose-written) separates cell generations; the number of peaks is chosen by BIC.
  • Statistical model fitting with uncertainties: an asymmetric binomial model of dye partitioning, with χ²/dof as goodness of fit.
  • Validation: the whole pipeline is tested on synthetic data with known ground truth.
  • Reproducible pipeline: driven by a TOML config, with tests.
Key result

Partitioning of total cytoplasm is close to symmetric: the split fraction p ranges from 0.516 ± 0.005 to 0.548 ± 0.003 across the four datasets (two weeks, live and fixed), and live and fixed cells agree. Size control is adder-like.

Validation on synthetic data revealed a small systematic bias in the estimated asymmetry (about +0.01): for p = 0.50 the pipeline recovered 0.511 ± 0.003.

Tools
Python: NumPy, pandas, SciPy, matplotlib; pytest; TOML configuration.
Links
Code on GitHub · Slides · Internship report (PDF)

In plain words: When a cell divides, its two daughters get almost equal shares of the cell's contents, and I built and tested the analysis that measures how equal the split is.

Actionable insight: Validating the pipeline on synthetic data with known ground truth exposed a bias of about +0.01 in the estimated asymmetry, so values just above 0.5 should be read with that in mind.
